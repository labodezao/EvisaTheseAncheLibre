# Reed blocks and plates

## A wooden comb under the reeds

A reed block is a wooden comb. Each tooth is a wall, and between two walls lies a chamber; a reed plate with its two tongues closes the chamber on one side, the soundboard with its hole on the other. On the instruments of the training courses the blocks are milled two by two from one blank, with their heels, and deburred before the course; the trainee checks that they fall in front of the holes of the soundboard, counter-drills the fixings, glues each block on its heels, waxes the plates into their places, checks the furnished block for leaks, and screws it onto the soundboard with M3 screws into threaded inserts.

What the shape of the chamber does to the sound, the volume of its cells, the hole, the coupling of the two voices of a note, is acoustics, and the thesis treats it. This chapter keeps what decides whether a block is right in the hands of the maker: that it is tight, that every reed has its room, and that what is screwed can be taken apart without changing the note.

### Flat, or not tight

Every face of a block is a flat joint: plate on wax, block on heel, block on soundboard. The cube law of the leak chapter applies to all of them, and it is severe. A joint of 100 mm around with 5 mm of wood to cross leaks 0.05 L/min at 500 Pa, the proposed figure for a whole block, with a gap of only 0.026 mm (@fig:joint). A block has several such joints, so each must do better. No milling machine and no hand plane leave two faces that close; something must fill the gap. That is the job of the wax under and around a plate, of the glue between block and heel, and of the gasket or the mastic between block and soundboard. Seen from the cube law, the choice between them is a choice of how a gap is filled, and of whether it can be filled again.

A glued block is tight by nature and does not come off. A screwed block comes off for tuning and repair, and then everything rests on its gasket: crushed once too often, the gasket no longer fills the gap, and the leak comes back with the cube of the lost hundredths. Wax around a plate seals and can be redone with a spoon; it softens with heat and hardens with age. Nails through a strip of leather hold the plate mechanically and leave the sealing to the leather. What these choices do to the tongue itself, its damping and its pitch, is a question of the thesis (the chapter on reed coupling), and it has no answer yet beyond the protocol that would give one.

### A leak between two chambers

There is a leak that the gasometer does not see, because it does not go outside. If the face of a block does not seal against the soundboard between two chambers, air goes from one chamber to its neighbour. When the pallet of one note opens, a little air reaches the next chamber, whose pallet is shut; if a reed there is close to its threshold, it sounds faintly. A ghost note on a neighbour, not on the note itself, points to this leak under the block rather than to a pallet.

### Each reed in its room

The booklet asks that no reed touch the bottom of its cell or the partition. The reason is plain: a tongue that grazes wood in its swing does not speak, or buzzes. The milling program checks the room of each reed on the model before cutting (a reed laid in its case does not touch the partition); the trainee checks it again after waxing, because a bead of wax can push a plate off its line.

## Making and checking the blocks

Each step of the course, with its reason.

**Sort the reeds by block, then by row, then by note,** each in front of its case marked A or B. A plate in the wrong case is a note in the wrong place, and once waxed it is a repair.

**Wax each plate,** the wax warmed in a bottle warmer and poured with a spoon all round the plate, the bead continuous. The bead is the filler of the cube law: a gap in it is a hole, and a hole of one millimetre is almost a litre per minute.

**Check the furnished block,** its slots closed with tape, on the gasometer, in pressure then in suction, because a plate can be pressed onto its wax in one direction and pulled away from it in the other. Smoke to find a leak. Proposed figure: under 0.05 L/min at 500 Pa.

**Measure the flow of each reed,** tape removed one at a time, by the descent of the gasometer at 100, 300 and 500 Pa. This is not an examination but a measurement: the air that reed consumes, which the bellows chapter needs and which nobody has yet.

**Screw the blocks onto the soundboard,** M3 screws into the inserts, then a lamp: no light between block and soundboard.

> To be completed (Ewen): the gasket or mastic between block and soundboard on your instruments, and how many times a screwed block can be taken off before its gasket must be changed.

## At the repair bench

**One note late and soft.** Tape its slot and test the block: if the leak goes when you press the plate, rewax it; if it stays, look at the chamber and its face on the soundboard.

**A neighbour that whispers.** A leak between two chambers under the block. Take the block off, look at the face against the soundboard and at the gasket; flatten the face on abrasive paper laid on glass if it is no longer plane.

**A note that changed after the blocks were taken off and put back.** The thesis asks whether remounting a block moves the pitch of its tongues by more than the few cents that the chamber itself moves them; the bench is where the answer will come from. Note the deviation of each note with the tuner before and after.

**A reed that buzzes.** It touches its cell, or its plate has moved in the wax. Look along the slot against a light, and rewax straight.

> To be completed (Ewen): when a screwed block is taken off and put back, does any tongue change pitch by more than a few cents? Three notes, with the tuner, before and after.
